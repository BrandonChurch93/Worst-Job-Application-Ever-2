## What this project is, in six lines

- A comedy told as a job application on a website. A careers page, then a five-step application that fights back, then instant rejection.
- Every page is a real, usable web page made of real things: matte off-white ceramic, frosted glass, brushed aluminum, glossy orange plastic. One soft light from the upper left. Seen straight on like a screenshot.
- The hero is the mouse cursor: a glossy white enamel arrow with a black edge, about 70 pixels tall on a 1920 by 1080 page. It acts like a cartoon character and is rendered like a product photograph. It has no face, no eyes, no legs.
- Step 3 of the application is the "Skills Assessment". It is a pinball machine. The cursor is the ball.
- It is one site delivered two ways: a film in which the site plays itself, and a live site a visitor plays with their own mouse. So the machine has to be something a web page could really hold and a visitor could really play.
- It gets built in three.js from these images. Clear, solid, buildable shapes beat a mood.

## The three changes

1. **The cursor is sealed inside a clear ball, and that ball is the pinball.** You always see the cursor inside it.
2. **The machine is big, and it is built into the page.** There is no cabinet on legs and no arcade room. Step 3 is a tall page. Its first screen holds the heading and one control, the loader port. The rest of the page, below, is the machine, set into the ceramic like a huge embedded component, nearly as wide as the page. The cursor is sucked into the clear ball at the port and sent down a clear tube to the plunger.
3. **The camera is free.** It pulls back to show the whole machine from the player's end, pushes in on the part where the ball is, follows it, and rides above it on the wire ramp looking down into the ball.

## Two tests every image must pass

**Test A. A pinball player would believe it.**

- Every part is a real pinball part, built the way real ones are: flippers on shafts with rubber, slingshots with rubber rings, pop bumpers with a skirt, a ring and a cap, drop targets, standup targets, a spinner, a scoop, posts with rubber rings, wire guides, lane guides, lit inserts set flush in the playfield, flasher domes, small lamps with coloured caps, screws with real heads.
- The layout works. Every lane is a ball and a half wide. Every ramp has a way on and a way off. The flippers can reach every shot. Nothing is fused, melted, floating or impossible. Nothing blocks a lane.
- It is crowded edge to edge and layered: plastics above wire above print, a wire ramp crossing over a lane, something behind everything. No bare stretch of playfield.
- Most of the detail of a real playfield is its printed artwork under a glossy clear coat. It is a flat board first. The buildings are small moulded toys standing on it among the hardware, the way sculpted toys stand on a real machine. It is not a model railway, not a diorama, not a toy city.
- The same parts are in the same places in every image.

**Test B. It belongs to this website.**

- Its frame is made of the website's materials: side rails and lockdown bar in the same brushed aluminum as the keyboard tray in `step1-fire.png`, a narrow border of the same matte off-white ceramic page all round it, and the website's glossy orange for the flippers, the plunger knob and the start button.
- Inside that frame it is its own world, with its own colours and its own light.
- No legs, no coin door, no floor, no room, no other machines. Beyond the edge of the page there is only darkness.
- Nothing hides behind fog, smoke, haze, glare or blur. In the three master views every part is sharp.

The two rejected tables failed test A: they were built out of the form's own grey parts, sparse and generic. A table that passes A and fails B would be an arcade photo with no connection to the site. Pass both.

## The clear ball

- A sphere of thick, perfectly clear acrylic, like a large gumball capsule. A fine seam round its middle. One small aluminum plug set flush in its side, where it was sealed.
- The cursor is inside it: the website's white enamel arrow with its black edge, a solid object, nearly filling the ball. Its length is about three quarters of the ball's width. It is never printed on the ball and never a reflection.
- It behaves like a thing loose in a ball. When the ball is still, the cursor lies against the bottom. When the ball rolls, the cursor tumbles a beat behind it. When the ball is hit, the cursor is thrown flat against the inside wall.
- It still acts: it sweats, and the drops bead on the inside of the acrylic. It goes pale. It presses itself against the far wall away from what is coming.
- The acrylic has crisp highlights from the table's lamps and bends what is behind it a little. It never hides the cursor.
- No liquid in it. It is not a snow globe. Nothing else is in it.
- The cursor has no face, no eyes, no mouth, no legs, no arms, no gloves.
- The other balls on the table are ordinary chrome steel pinballs, the same size, mirror bright.

## Light

- The frame, the ceramic border and the page in G3 are lit by the website's light: one large soft source from the upper left, clean, no window shapes, no bands. In the machine images it is turned down to a dusk level.
- The machine is lit by its own lamps: warm general lighting round its edges, a lamp under every insert, the flasher domes, a lamp in each bumper, lit windows in every building, the backbox. Light spills from an insert onto the post beside it. The clear coat on the playfield shows sharp reflections of lamps and parts.
- No glass sheet over the playfield in any image.

## Method

1. Write the start time into `references/drafts/round-6/NOTES.md` before anything else.
2. Study first. Find up to six photographs of real modern arcade pinball machines: two playfields from directly above, two from the player's end, two close views of hardware such as a pop bumper, a wire ramp and lit inserts. Study how they are built, how crowded they are and how they are lit. Take no name, no artwork and no layout from any of them. List their sources in the notes. Do not commit them.
3. Use the strongest photoreal image model you have that accepts reference images, and the same one for all eleven. Record which.
4. Order: G2, then G1, then G4, then G3, then G5 to G11.
5. Pass the unlabeled guide for a view into the generation as a reference image, and say in the prompt that it is a layout guide of flat coloured blocks: follow where things are and how big they are, and make everything a real, photographed object. Never pass a labeled guide.
6. From G1 on, pass the kept take of G2 as a reference. From G4 on, pass the kept takes of G1 and G2. From G5 on, also pass the kept take of G4.
7. Takes: G1, G2 and G4 get at most four each. Every other image gets at most three. Retries count. At the limit, keep the best take and move on.
8. Look at every take yourself against "What to check" below, before anything else. A take that fails a line marked **must** is made again inside the limit, with the prompt changed to fix that line.
9. Then one fresh agent that has not seen this brief judges the take blind. Give it the image and exactly these two questions: "Which is this most likely to be: a frame from a top-budget film or a photograph by a professional, a stock image, a textbook picture, or amateur generated art? And: could a ball really travel every lane and ramp you can see? Name anything that could not work." Only the first answer to the first question, with nothing named in the second, passes. Make a failed take again inside the limit. Give the judge ten minutes and one replacement, then keep the take unjudged.
10. Show each finished image in this thread as soon as it is done, displayed inline so Brandon sees it without downloading a file. Do not wait for the set. Push after every finished image.
11. If a tool or an agent fails three times, skip it, note it and move on.
12. Finish with a contact sheet of the kept take of each image at `references/drafts/round-6/contact-sheet.jpg`, and complete `NOTES.md`.

Work for at most three hours from the start time you wrote down. When every image has its kept take or its limit, or the time is up, push what exists and end the task with: `REFERENCES READY`

## What to check in every take

- **Must.** It reads at a glance as a real pinball machine a person could play.
- **Must.** No people, no hands, no faces. No face, eyes, mouth, legs or arms on the cursor.
- **Must.** No real machine's name, artwork, characters or logos. No brand of any kind.
- **Must.** No legs under the machine, no arcade room, no floor, no other machines.
- **Must.** In every image that has the ball: the cursor is inside it, a solid white arrow with a black edge, clearly seen.
- **Must, G1, G2 and G11.** `THE DAILY GRIND` is spelled right, or the panel is blank.
- **Must, G1 and G2.** The flippers are at the foot, the tower is at the head, and the layout follows the guide.
- **Must, G3.** It passes as a page of the same website as `page-look.png`: straight on, sharp, the same ceramic, aluminum and orange.
- The count in G2 holds. Say which counts miss.
- Every word in the image is one of the allowed words, spelled right. List every wrong or extra word.
- The frame is brushed aluminum with a ceramic border, and the flippers are orange.
- Nothing is fused, floating or impossible. Every ramp has a way on and a way off.
- The same buildings are in the same places as in G2.

## Never

- A cabinet on legs, a coin door, an arcade, a carpet, a wall, a window.
- A toy, a diorama, a model railway, an isometric game picture, a flat illustration, a cartoon render.
- Fog, smoke, haze, lens flares that hide parts, motion blur in G1, G2, G3 or G4.
- A hamster ball, a snow globe, a marble with a picture in it.
- A hand cursor, a text cursor, a cursor with a face.
- Words that are not on the allowed list.
